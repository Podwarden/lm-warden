Self-hosting has eight problems. Here is what each one looks like solved.

A bare engine serves one model on one port. These are the parts around it, with our own numbers where we have them.

  1. Will this model fit my GPU?

    Paste a Hugging Face repo into Add model. Every file is listed with a verdict against the cards you tick: fits, tight headroom, or won’t fit. Tick more cards and the budget adds up. Nothing downloads until something fits.

  2. You bought GPUs. Are they working or waiting?

    Throughput is concurrency × prompt length × cache reuse, and a bare engine shows none of it together. The Stats page does, over 1 h to 7 d: VRAM, GPU utilisation, power (Linux), tokens per second with prompt tokens split into cached and computed, p50/p95 latency, prompt-cache hit rate, preemptions, requests per key and per client IP. Flat utilisation with a queue says “more concurrency”; a low cache share says “pin sessions”.

  3. Talk to the model before you wire anything up.

    The chat is in the same window as everything else. Streaming Markdown with highlighted code and maths; up to eight PNG, JPEG or WebP images per message for a vision model; temperature, top-p, max tokens and system prompt per chat, saved as defaults; chats kept, titled, forkable. Tool calls the model makes are rendered, so you can check tool calling before an agent depends on it.

  4. What did the model actually see, and say?

    God mode, for debugging prompts: with VW_GODMODE_ENABLED on the deployment, each key’s page gets a dock that streams that key’s prompts and completions live. Off by default, and absent when off. A bounded in-memory ring: nothing to the database or disk, gone on restart. Anyone with the admin session can open it; the warden has one admin role and no others.

Every agent turn re-reads the whole conversation.

An agent resends its whole history every turn, so a long conversation is mostly a prefix the engine has already read. vLLM keeps that work in its prefix cache, but only on the replica that did it, and its own balancer sends each turn to whichever replica has the shortest queue. LM Warden places each new conversation on the least-loaded replica and keeps it there, using the session id Claude Code already sends. It moves a request only when that replica passes its spill threshold.

Measured on our four-card box (4 × RTX A4000, Qwen3-4B as four one-card replicas, synthetic Claude Code-style sessions, affinity off then on, back to back): with conversations pinned, the share of prompt tokens served from the cache rose from 56 to 86% at 4 concurrent sessions, and the median time to first byte fell from 2.05 to 0.38 s. Output tokens a minute were higher at every step we ran, and the p95 time to first byte (the slowest 5%) was lower at every step. The number of replicas doing work barely moved (2.71 against 2.83 of four at 4 sessions): the gain is reuse of the cache, not more cards working.

Every step of the ramp, affinity off → on

{{fig:t1-ramp}}

Measured on our four-card box with a small model, affinity off first, then on, without restarting vLLM in between: the second arm started with a warm prefix cache, and both arms drew their prompts from the same seed. Each step lasts 3 minutes, so it completes only 9 to 31 sessions and one session more or less moves sessions an hour by 20; output tokens a minute is the steadier column. Your model, cards and workload will differ; the replica card on a model’s page shows the same counters for yours.

Where it loses. The 24 and 32 session steps are a deliberate overload of four small cards, and every replica is saturated. Output is still higher (11,178 against 9,048 and 16,638 against 14,760 tokens a minute) and the slow tail is much shorter (p95 38 against 59 s, and 39 against 83 s), but the median time to first byte is worse with affinity: 13.1 against 5.1 s at 24 sessions, 27.7 against 12.3 s at 32. We have not isolated why, and lowering the spill threshold from 8 to 4 did not change it.

Measure both on a busy box. The advantage is largest while there is headroom; a box that runs at its limit should measure both settings, and affinity can be switched off per model. Replicas, and so this routing, are part of the Linux install with NVIDIA GPUs; the Mac app (beta) has no tensor or data parallelism.

Let the AI you already pay for tune your own GPUs.

Choosing a model for your cards means choosing a quantisation, an engine, how to split it across GPUs, how much context to give it and the KV-cache type; each guess costs a download and a load. Everything the console does is an API call, so Claude Code, Codex or another agent with a shell can run that search for you, on your hardware, against your workload.

The loop

What the agent calls: GET /api/system/gpus, POST /api/models/fit-preview before any download, register and suggest-config, pull and load, a stress run that finds and applies the real context ceiling, GET /api/stats/v2/throughput and latency, and an inference key it issues to send test traffic. It reads /agent-guide.md first, a short description of these calls written for a language model.

Four prompts to paste, and what comes back

  1. Survey and shortlist. Ask: list my GPUs, shortlist three open-weight models for agentic coding that fit with at least 65,536 tokens of context, check each with fit-preview, download nothing. You get: a table of model, quantisation, engine, GPUs, context, and why.
  2. Engine and layout for one model. Ask: decide vLLM or llama.cpp and tensor parallel or data-parallel replicas on my cards; load the best two layouts, send each the same 20 streaming requests, compare at concurrency 1 and 8. You get: time to first token and tokens per second for both, the winner left loaded, the other unloaded, the test key revoked.
  3. Find the real limits and apply them. Ask: stress-run the loaded model, apply the measured context, then benchmark at concurrency 1, 4 and 16 with prompts of about 2k and 30k tokens. You get: the context ceiling written into the model’s settings and a table of time to first token and tokens per second per concurrency and prompt size.
  4. Compare two candidates on your own work. Ask: replay the prompts in ./workload/*.txt against model A and model B at concurrency 4, judge each answer. You get: model-comparison.md with latency, tokens per second, correctness by the agent’s judgement and a recommendation; the recommended model stays loaded.

Before you start. An admin token has full admin rights over the warden (models, keys, settings; there are no narrower scopes yet): give it a short expiry and revoke it in Settings → Access Tokens when the job is done. A stress run deliberately crashes an engine to find its limit: run these prompts on a warden nobody else depends on. Downloads are large; the prompts check fit before pulling, but watch your disk.

Engine and layout choices (vLLM, tensor or data parallel) are the Linux install; on a Mac the choice is MLX or llama.cpp on one GPU. The model doing the tuning can be whichever you already pay for; the model it tunes is the one on your cards. The agent-tuning page: the four prompts in full.

One GPU box, shared by your whole team, your family and your agents.

Create as many API keys as you need and hand them out: a colleague, a family member, a CI job, an agent farm, each with its own key, its own priority and its own usage page. The hardware works for everyone, and the important work is never kept waiting.

Strict priority: 9 is served first, 0 last

A waiting higher-priority request always goes before a lower one. Under load, the capacity left after the important keys is what the lower keys get; on a busy box a low priority can wait indefinitely. Default is 5. There is no per-key rate limit; priority replaced it.

  1. P9
    The production agentalways answered first
  2. P7
    Your own coding sessionsnext in line
  3. P5
    Colleagues’ keysthe default
  4. P3
    Family and hobby projectswhatever is left
  5. P1
    Overnight batch jobswait for idle time

An example ladder, not a rule: you choose the numbers. Each key can also be paused (new requests refused while the running ones finish), rotated with a grace window, and restricted to the models it may call.

Who is using the box right now, and how, is on the same pages: every request in flight with its key, session and phase, a per-key table on the Stats page, and the Stats panels for the whole host.

Your hardware, honestly: from a Mac to eight B300s.

The same console reads a 16 GB M1, two mismatched cards under a desk, and an 8 × B300 NVSwitch server, and it names the bottleneck instead of averaging it away: a PCIe link that settled at x4 of x16, NVLink links that are inactive, ECC that differs between cards.

What each panel shows per card: temperature against its own throttle point, power against its cap, clocks, fan, the PCIe generation and width as negotiated right now, ECC and NVLink when the card has them, and the process holding the memory. The interconnect drawing is read from nvidia-smi topo -m. On a Mac: GPU use and the memory budget Metal reports, without power. And the engine under all of it is watched by its own /health, not its process table: a dead engine under a live wrapper is reloaded with its evidence kept.
